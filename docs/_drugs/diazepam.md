## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism-of-action data for diazepam were not available in the data compiled for this report. The published literature in that data describes diazepam as a positive allosteric modulator of the GABA-A receptor. It increases the effect of the brain's main inhibitory neurotransmitter, which produces sedative and anxiolytic effects.

Dampening neuronal excitability in this way is a recognised route to sleep induction, and benzodiazepines have long been used as hypnotics. The mechanism is therefore plausible for insomnia.

There are important limits. Diazepam is long-acting and has active metabolites (such as nordiazepam). These can cause residual daytime sedation and accumulation. Tolerance, dependence and disruption of sleep architecture also limit long-term use. Much of the retrieved literature concerns benzodiazepines as a class, herbal medicines, or the harms of chronic use, rather than diazepam-specific efficacy.

## Safety Considerations

- **Drug Interactions:** The interaction data compiled for this report list 100 interacting drugs. Examples:
  - **Major:** morphine (additive central nervous system and respiratory depression).
  - **Moderate:** bupropion, aprepitant, omeprazole, esomeprazole, cimetidine, clarithromycin, cisapride, clotrimazole, miconazole, dronabinol, metoclopramide.
  - **Minor:** dexamethasone, betamethasone, and several antacids (magnesium oxide, aluminium hydroxide, calcium carbonate).
- **Literature signals:** Long-term benzodiazepine use is linked to dependence, memory impairment, falls, residual sedation and possible cancer risk (see the literature table).

Label warnings and contraindications were not available in the data compiled for this report. Please refer to the SmPC and BNF for full safety information. Report suspected adverse reactions via the Yellow Card Scheme.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The mechanism is plausible and the model score is very high. However, the only direct evidence is one small 1981 comparative study in which diazepam performed worse than the comparator. Diazepam is not a first-line hypnotic, and its long-acting metabolites and dependence risk weigh against it. Evidence Level is L2.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Placebo-controlled or active-comparator trials of diazepam in insomnia that use modern outcome measures.
- Detailed mechanism-of-action data and label-based warnings and contraindications.
- A comparison against current first-line options, including a risk-benefit assessment for dependence, next-day impairment and use in older adults.
- Confirmation of the approved indication and product details from the full SmPC.
