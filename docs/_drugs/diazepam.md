## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Diazepam is a positive allosteric modulator of GABA-A receptors. By enhancing inhibitory neurotransmission it produces sedative-hypnotic effects, and benzodiazepines as a class are used clinically as hypnotics. This gives the prediction a plausible, well-established mechanistic basis. Detailed mechanism-of-action data for diazepam were not available in the data compiled for this report, so this explanation rests on general pharmacology.

There are important limits. Diazepam is not a first-line hypnotic: its long-acting active metabolite (nordiazepam) causes residual daytime sedation, and tolerance, dependence and disruption of sleep architecture restrict long-term use. The supplied literature does not include diazepam-specific efficacy trials for insomnia, and no cited study meets the standard for a direct, disease-specific evidence level above L5.

## Safety Considerations

- **Drug Interactions**: 100 interactions were found in the interaction data. Examples include a Major interaction with morphine (additive central nervous system and respiratory depression is the main concern with opioids), and Moderate interactions with bupropion, aprepitant, omeprazole, esomeprazole, cimetidine, clarithromycin, cisapride, clotrimazole, dronabinol, metoclopramide and miconazole.
- **Conditions of concern in the interaction/safety data**: Contraindication-type relationships were recorded for liver failure and liver disease, respiratory failure, sleep apnoea syndromes, myasthenia gravis, open-angle and angle-closure glaucoma, and substance-related disorders. These do not come from label text and should be checked against the SmPC.
- **Literature safety signals**: Long-term benzodiazepine use is associated with dependence, tolerance, cognitive decline, falls in older adults and disruption of sleep architecture.

Please refer to the SmPC and BNF for full safety information. Report suspected adverse reactions via the Yellow Card Scheme.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The mechanism is plausible and benzodiazepines are used as hypnotics, but no clinical trials are registered and no cited study directly shows diazepam efficacy in insomnia. The literature largely highlights the harms of long-term use, and other agents are preferred for insomnia.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Diazepam-specific randomised evidence in insomnia against current standard care
- Detailed mechanism-of-action data
- Review of SmPC warnings, contraindications and interaction data for the UK product
- A risk assessment for dependence, residual sedation and use in older adults
