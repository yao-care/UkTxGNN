## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data are not available in the data compiled for this report. Ciprofloxacin belongs to the fluoroquinolone class, which is generally understood to inhibit bacterial DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV. That is an antibacterial mechanism, and it does not directly explain an effect on scleroderma.

Two indirect links are suggested in the literature:

- A small study proposed that oral ciprofloxacin may have an antifibrotic effect on the skin of people with scleroderma. The mechanism is not established, and the abstract available to us does not report results.
- In systemic sclerosis, small bowel bacterial overgrowth can cause diarrhoea and malabsorption. An antibacterial agent may plausibly help these gut symptoms, which is a symptomatic effect rather than a treatment of the underlying disease.

Neither study directly demonstrates that ciprofloxacin treats diffuse scleroderma, so the prediction remains a hypothesis.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
No cited study directly shows that ciprofloxacin is effective for diffuse scleroderma, so the evidence level is L5 on model prediction alone. The related reports are small or indirect, and the safety profile includes tendon, neuropathy and QT-related concerns that matter in a chronic autoimmune population.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Published outcome data from the 2010 antifibrotic study, with critical appraisal of its design and results
- Mechanism of action data and a biological rationale for antifibrotic activity
- A registered, adequately powered randomised trial specifically in diffuse scleroderma
- Safety assessment for long-term or repeated fluoroquinolone use in scleroderma, including tendon, neurological and cardiac risks
- Local licence indication text and SmPC warnings to complete the regulatory picture
