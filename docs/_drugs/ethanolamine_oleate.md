# Ethanolamine Oleate: From Sclerotherapy of Oesophageal Varices to Oesophageal Disease

## One-Sentence Summary

Ethanolamine oleate is a sclerosing agent historically administered by endoscopic injection to control bleeding from oesophageal and gastric varices, though it currently holds no UK marketing authorisation. Of the ten candidate indications generated by TxGNN, only **oesophageal disease** (i.e., variceal sclerotherapy) is backed by meaningful evidence — **3 clinical trials** and **20 publications**, including several randomised controlled trials — while the model's higher-scoring "novel" predictions (e.g., non-syndromic oesophageal malformation, immune thrombocytopenia) were reviewed in the evidence pack and found to have no plausible mechanistic or clinical support.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism-of-action data is not available for ethanolamine oleate in this evidence pack (flagged as a High-severity data gap). Based on known pharmacology, ethanolamine oleate is a classic chemical sclerosant: intravariceal injection induces local chemical damage to the venous endothelium, triggering thrombosis and subsequent fibrosis that obliterates the varix. This mechanism has underpinned endoscopic injection sclerotherapy (EIS) for decades.

Because the "new" indication — oesophageal disease — sits in the same organ system and pathophysiological space (portal hypertension leading to oesophageal/gastric variceal bleeding) as the drug's known clinical use, this is best understood as the evidence base **re-confirming an established use** rather than a genuine repurposing opportunity. The clinical trial and literature evidence below directly compares ethanolamine oleate against other sclerosants (polidocanol, absolute ethanol, N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate) and against newer techniques (band ligation, balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration), all within variceal bleeding management.

By contrast, the mechanistic rationale for the higher-scoring novel candidates does not hold up: structural congenital oesophageal malformations are anatomical defects unrelated to a chemical sclerosant's mode of action, and the platelet/coagulation disorders (Scott syndrome, Glanzmann thrombasthenia, pseudo-von Willebrand disease) have no established connection to endothelial sclerosis. The one partial exception — immune thrombocytopenia — has a single mechanistic paper on a *related* sclerosant (sodium morrhuate) showing granulocyte activation and endothelial/erythrocyte damage, but this is indirect, does not involve ethanolamine oleate itself, and does not establish a therapeutic (rather than adverse) effect.
