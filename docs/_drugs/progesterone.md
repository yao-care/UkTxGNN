## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Based on known pharmacology, progesterone is the natural progestogen. It works with oestradiol to control the cyclical proliferation and shedding of the endometrium, and it influences the pulsatile secretion of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), LH and FSH.

Withdrawal of progesterone triggers endometrial shedding. For this reason it is used diagnostically (the progestogen challenge) and therapeutically in secondary amenorrhoea caused by anovulation or low-oestrogen states. The trials identified are in hypothalamic amenorrhoea and functional hypogonadism (athletes, anorexia nervosa). In these settings, oestrogen with cyclical progestogen is studied mainly for bone, metabolic and neurobiological outcomes.

Amenorrhoea is already close to an existing use of progesterone, so this is not a truly novel repurposing. It is better seen as an extension of established hormonal practice.

## Safety Considerations

**Contraindications suggested by the knowledge graph (not from label text):** the TxGNN knowledge graph links progesterone to the following conditions as contraindications. These must be verified against the SmPC.
- Cardiovascular and metabolic: hypertension, diabetes mellitus (including diabetic ketoacidosis), hyperglycaemia, hypertriglyceridaemia
- Hepatic and renal: liver failure, liver disease, gallbladder disease, kidney disease
- Neurological and psychiatric: epilepsy, anxiety and neurotic disorders, dysthymic disorder, substance abuse or dependence
- Metabolic and endocrine: porphyria, hypothyroidism
- Other: asthma, systemic lupus erythematosus, osteoporosis, humoral hypercalcaemia of malignancy

**Drug interactions:** 100 interactions were recorded, and the 20 listed in the data compiled for this report were all of moderate severity. They are mainly with glucose-lowering agents (acarbose, metformin, pioglitazone, sulfonylureas such as glimepiride and chlorpropamide, DPP-4 inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 agonists and insulins), which may reflect effects on glucose control. Others include aprepitant and clarithromycin, which may alter progesterone metabolism.

Please refer to the SmPC and BNF for full safety information. Report suspected adverse reactions via the Yellow Card Scheme.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Proceed with Guardrails**

**Rationale:**
One completed Phase 3 trial and one completed Phase 2/3 trial exist in relevant populations, and the mechanism is well understood. However, progesterone is only part of the hormone regimen in these studies, no trial isolates its effect on amenorrhoea, and the new indication is close to current practice.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Trial data isolating progesterone's contribution, such as a comparison of cyclic progestogen with no progestogen on resumption of menses
- Confirmed UK licensed indications, dosage form and SmPC safety text, as the label data are missing
- A formal mechanism of action entry
- Screening against the knowledge-graph contraindications and the interactions with glucose-lowering drugs

**Other predicted indications (for context):**
- **Breast fibrocystic disease, cervix endometriosis, rectovaginal septum and vaginal endometriosis, and endometriosis in a cutaneous scar** have only descriptive or case-report evidence and no clinical trials (research questions only).
- **Benign mammary dysplasia, bilateral renal hypoplasia, renal hypoplasia, blunt duct adenosis and apocrine adenosis of breast** have no credible mechanistic or clinical support (Hold).
