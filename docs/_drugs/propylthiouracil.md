# Propylthiouracil: From Hyperthyroidism to Resistance to Thyroid Hormone (TRβ Mutation)

## One-Sentence Summary

Propylthiouracil is an antithyroid drug originally used to treat hyperthyroidism, Graves disease, thyrotoxicosis and thyroid crisis.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **resistance to thyroid hormone due to a mutation in thyroid hormone receptor beta**, but **no clinical trials** and only **4 publications** (animal models and case reports) were found, and none shows a treatment benefit.
The evidence is weak, and the mechanistic rationale is arguably counterproductive.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism-of-action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Based on known pharmacology, propylthiouracil inhibits thyroid peroxidase and so reduces thyroid hormone synthesis. Peripherally, it also inhibits type 1 deiodinase, which converts T4 to T3. Its efficacy in hyperthyroidism is established.

The predicted indication is, however, mechanistically doubtful. In resistance to thyroid hormone beta (RTH-β), tissues respond poorly to T3, and the pituitary-thyroid axis compensates with raised TSH, T4 and T3. Lowering hormone output with an antithyroid drug would be expected to raise TSH further, and so worsen goitre and pituitary stimulation. The high TxGNN score most likely reflects network proximity to thyroid hormone biology rather than a true therapeutic fit.

The literature supports this caution. One case report describes a patient with RTH whose goitre enlarged after propylthiouracil (PMID 10724359). Another series describes children with partial hormone resistance who were treated for suspected thyrotoxicosis and had poor TSH suppression afterwards (PMID 3097618).

By contrast, the second-ranked prediction, **neonatal thyrotoxicosis**, has a strong mechanistic link, as the disease mechanism is the same as in maternal Graves disease. However, most of the evidence concerns treating the mother to protect the fetus and neonate, which extends the existing indication. Methimazole is commonly preferred outside the first trimester because of propylthiouracil's hepatotoxicity risk.

## Safety Considerations

**Contraindication signals** (from knowledge-graph drug-disease links, not label text): thrombocytopenia, hypothyroidism, myxedema, congenital hypothyroidism, hyperbilirubinemia (including Gilbert syndrome), interstitial lung disease, drug-induced liver injury, and a range of blood disorders (leukopenia, pancytopenia, agranulocytosis, neutropenia and congenital neutropenia syndromes).

**Drug interactions:** 83 interactions were recorded. The main ones by severity are:
- **Major:** deferiprone
- **Moderate:** warfarin, dicoumarol, theophylline, aminophylline, oxtriphylline, naltrexone, radioactive iodine (I-131 and I-123), macimorelin

Other recorded interactions have unclassified severity (e.g. omeprazole, pantoprazole, clopidogrel, corticosteroids, rosuvastatin).

**Additional points from the literature:** hepatotoxicity risk and placental transfer with neonatal hepatitis (PMID 2090674).

Please refer to the SmPC and BNF for full safety information. Report suspected adverse reactions via the Yellow Card Scheme.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The top prediction has no clinical trials, and the supporting literature consists of mouse models and case reports, with some suggesting propylthiouracil may worsen RTH. Lowering thyroid hormone output in a condition with compensatory TSH elevation is mechanistically counterproductive. Neonatal thyrotoxicosis has a stronger rationale, but it largely overlaps with the existing hyperthyroidism use and is better handled as a research question than as a new indication.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Mechanism of action data and SmPC warnings and contraindications (the label safety review is a blocking gap)
- Confirmation of the authorised indication wording, dosage form and PL number for the UK product
- For neonatal thyrotoxicosis: comparative evidence against methimazole, with a hepatotoxicity risk assessment
- For hyperthyroxinemia: stratification by cause, as only true hormone overproduction is plausibly suitable
