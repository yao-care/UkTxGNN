# Acyclovir: From Herpes Simplex Infection to Common Wart

## One-Sentence Summary

Acyclovir is an antiviral drug originally used to treat herpes simplex, herpes zoster (shingles), chickenpox, herpes labialis, herpes simplex encephalitis and genital herpes.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **common wart** (intralesional use), with **4 registered clinical trials** and **19 publications** currently supporting this direction.
The evidence is small-scale and mostly from single centres, so it is best viewed as a research question rather than a ready-to-adopt therapy.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Acyclovir is a well-known guanosine analogue. In herpesvirus-infected cells it is activated by viral thymidine kinase and interferes with viral DNA synthesis. Its efficacy in herpes infections is well established.

Warts are caused by human papillomavirus (HPV), not a herpesvirus, and acyclovir is not a classical HPV antiviral. Proposed explanations for the observed benefit include interference with viral DNA synthesis and an immunomodulatory effect from intralesional injection (a possible local inflammatory response). These mechanisms remain hypotheses.

Several small randomised or comparative studies of intralesional acyclovir exist. They compare it with cryotherapy, 5-fluorouracil, Candida antigen, vitamin D3, HPV vaccine, PPD and hepatitis B vaccine. Sample sizes are small and heterogeneity is high, so this is repurposing-grade evidence that needs confirmation in larger trials.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Proceed with Guardrails** (for common wart, as a research question only)

**Rationale:**
Four registered trials (including one Phase 2/3 RCT with a published report) and several small comparative studies suggest intralesional acyclovir may help recalcitrant warts. However, trials are small, mostly single-centre, and the mechanism is unclear. Most of the other high-scoring predictions have no supportive clinical data and should be held.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Larger, multicentre, adequately powered RCTs against standard care (such as salicylic acid and cryotherapy), with long-term recurrence follow-up
- Clarification of the mechanism of action in HPV-related lesions
- Safety data and a dosing standard for intralesional administration, since this is an unlicensed route
- Complete SmPC and BNF warnings and contraindications, which were missing from the data compiled
- Review of the knowledge-graph contraindication signals against the label
- Confirmation of UK licence numbers and indication text, which were absent from the authorisation records
