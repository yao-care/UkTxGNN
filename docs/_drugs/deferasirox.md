## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism-of-action data are not available in the data compiled for this report. Deferasirox is known as an iron chelator, so the rationale rests on iron biology rather than on a documented mechanism.

Intracellular iron availability supports HIV-1 replication. A 2021 preclinical study (PMID 34550543) reports that iron in endolysosomes restricts Tat-mediated activation of the HIV-1 long terminal repeat (LTR) promoter, by promoting Tat oligomerisation and increasing β-catenin expression. This suggests a plausible but indirect link between iron homeostasis and HIV transcription.

The direction of effect is unclear. Depending on context, chelating iron could suppress, or could alter in an unhelpful way, viral replication. The high TxGNN score is a computational prediction only and is not evidence of clinical benefit.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The prediction rests on a high model score and one indirect preclinical study. There are no clinical trials and no study of deferasirox in HIV, and the direction of any effect is uncertain.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- In vitro and in vivo studies testing deferasirox directly against HIV-1 replication
- Mechanism-of-action data to clarify whether iron chelation would help or harm
- Review of safety in people living with HIV, including interactions with antiretroviral therapy and renal and hepatic risk
- Complete SmPC warnings and contraindications for the UK-licensed product
